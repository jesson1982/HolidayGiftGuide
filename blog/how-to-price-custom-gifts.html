How to Price Custom Gifts

Updated: January 8, 2026 | 16 min read | By James Rodriguez

Short answer: Price a custom gift by stacking every real cost, the blank product, printing, shipping, platform fees, and your time, then add a target margin of about forty percent on top. Start from the landed cost rather than from a rival's price, because underpricing hides the shipping and fee layers that quietly eat profit. Raise prices when material or ad costs climb, when your reviews let you, and before peak season so margins survive the rush. A simple spreadsheet that totals the lines below keeps your shop honest.

Bottom line: Build the price from cost up, not from what competitors charge, and revisit the sheet every quarter so slow months still pay you.

Start from landed cost, not rival prices

New sellers often open a competitor's store, copy the number, and wonder why the month ends in the red. The rival may buy blanks in bulk, pay lower platform fees, or absorb shipping to win volume. Your price has to cover your own stacked costs, and those are almost never identical to someone else's, no matter how similar the product looks.

Landed cost is the full amount it takes to put one finished gift in the customer's hands. It includes the blank, the print, the pack, the postage, and every percentage a platform takes before the money reaches you. Until you know that total, any price is a guess, and guessing is how custom gift shops drift into losing orders they thought were wins.

Write the number down per product, because a custom throw pillow cover and a weighted blanket do not share a cost structure. A simple per-item sheet removes the temptation to average everything into one price that overcharges the cheap item and subsidizes the expensive one. Precision here is what separates a hobby from a business that survives a slow quarter.

The cost lines you must count

The blank product is the floor. A premium fleece blanket blank costs more than a basic throw, and a weighted fill adds both material and postage weight. Printing is the next line, charged per panel or per color area depending on the method, and it rises with size and with photo detail that needs proofing time.

Shipping is the line sellers forget most often. A heavy or large item can cost more to post than to print, and international rates swing hard. Platform fees sit on top of the sale as a percentage plus a fixed cut, and payment processing takes another slice before net profit appears. Your time for design support and messages is the final line many shops leave at zero.

The share column is a guide, not a rule, because your volumes and niche change the balance, and a handmade detail line can justify a higher total than a mass-market rival charges.

Setting a target margin

Margin is what stays after every cost line is paid, and it is the only number that pays your rent in a slow month. A target around forty percent of the final price is a sane starting point for custom gifts, because the category carries returns, proofs, and seasonal swings that a fixed-margin commodity store does not face.

Do not confuse margin with markup. A forty percent margin means the cost is sixty percent of the price, which is a markup of roughly sixty-seven percent on cost. Sellers who set a markup instead of a margin drift downward as fees rise, because the percentage taken grows while the dollars left shrink. Anchor to margin and the math stays stable.

Protect the margin when you discount. A holiday sale that cuts twenty percent off the price can erase the entire profit if you started thin. Rather than slash the listed price, bundle a second item or offer free shipping above a threshold, which hides the discount inside a larger order and keeps the per-unit margin where it needs to be.

When to raise your prices

Raise prices when your input costs move, not when you feel greedy. A blank supplier increase, a shipping rate jump, or an ad-cost climb all eat margin silently until a month closes short. Passing a part of that through promptly is normal business, and customers who value a personalized gift accept small moves far better than a sudden large one later.

Reviews are your permission slip. Once buyers describe the item as worth more than they paid, you have room to lift the price toward the value they feel. Move in small steps, watch conversion, and stop when sales dip more than the higher margin gains. The data tells you the ceiling faster than any forum guess ever will, so trust the curve.

Beat the peak rather than chase it. Raise before the holiday rush so the higher price funds the faster production and support you will need, then hold steady through the season. A mid-December increase looks like gouging and hurts trust, while an October move simply funds a better product and arrives as the new normal for shoppers.

Levers that protect profit

The fastest lever is minimum order value. Offer free shipping only above a threshold that covers the postage, which nudges buyers to add a second gift and lifts the average ticket without raising sticker prices. Bundles of a pillow and a graphic t-shirt also spread the fixed platform fee across two items instead of one, improving margin per order.

The second lever is proof efficiency. Tight design rules and clear upload guides cut the hours you spend fixing files, and that time is real cost even when it never appears on a supplier invoice. Shops that standardize their artwork flow quietly out-earn those that hand-hold every order, because the saved hours compound across hundreds of sales a year.

Channel choice is the third lever. Selling where fees are lower or where your niche already gathers reduces the percentage taken before profit. The list below sums the moves we see work best for small custom gift shops trying to keep margins healthy through both busy and quiet months of the year.

  • Set free shipping above a threshold that covers the true postage cost.
  • Bundle a pillow with a tee to spread the fixed platform fee.
  • Standardize artwork rules to cut the hours spent on proofs.
  • Raise prices in small steps when reviews show room to move.
  • Sell where fees are lower or your niche already gathers.

Pricing for slow months

Custom gifts are seasonal, and the quarter after a holiday can feel empty. The margin you banked in peak season is what carries payroll and software through the lull, which is why a thin margin is riskier than it looks on a good December afternoon. Price for the year, not for the week.

Build a small buffer into every price so a 10 percent volume drop does not become a loss. That buffer also funds the experiments, a new blank color, a fresh design drop, that refill the pipeline before the next peak. Shops without the buffer freeze and miss the window while competitors ship.

Watch the sheet quarterly, because supplier and ad costs drift and the old price quietly stops covering them. A ten-minute review each season beats a painful reset after a bad quarter. Treat pricing as maintenance, like restocking, and the shop stays profitable instead of surprising you with a number you did not see coming.

A simple pricing worksheet

Open a spreadsheet with one row per cost line and one column for your real supplier quotes. Total the rows, divide by your target margin complement, and the result is the floor price you should list. Anything lower loses money once a return or a reprint appears, and those always appear eventually in a custom category.

Test the floor against the market. If it lands above rivals by a lot, either your costs are too high or your value is unproven, and you fix the cheaper problem first. If it lands below, you may be leaving margin on the table that better photos and clearer copy would let you keep without losing sales.

Save a version per product and date it, because next quarter's shipping rate will not match this one. The discipline of one living sheet is what turns guesswork into a price you can defend to an accountant, a partner, or yourself when a slow month asks whether the shop actually makes money.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much margin should I target on custom gifts?

Aim for about forty percent margin, meaning costs are sixty percent of the price. Custom gifts carry proofs, returns, and seasonal swings, so a thin margin leaves nothing for a slow quarter. Anchor to margin rather than markup, because fees rise as a percentage and a markup-based price drifts downward as costs climb over the year.

Why should I price from cost instead of competitors?

Rivals may buy blanks in bulk, pay lower fees, or absorb shipping to win volume, so their price rarely matches your real cost. Pricing from your own landed cost keeps every order profitable. Copying a competitor's number hides the shipping and fee layers that quietly turn a sale into a loss you only see at month end.

When is the right time to raise prices?

Raise when input costs move, when reviews show buyers feel the item is worth more, and before peak season rather than during it. Small steps protect conversion better than one large jump. An October increase funds faster production, while a December increase reads as gouging and hurts the trust you built.

How do I discount without killing my margin?

Avoid cutting the sticker price, which can erase profit on a thin margin. Instead offer free shipping above a threshold or bundle a second item, which hides the discount inside a larger order. Bundles also spread the fixed platform fee across two products, so the per-unit margin stays healthier through the promotion.

What costs do new sellers forget to include?

Shipping weight, platform and payment fees, and their own time for design support are the usual omissions. A heavy or large item can cost more to post than to print, and international rates swing hard. Listing your time at zero hides a real cost, so build every line into the price before you set the number.
